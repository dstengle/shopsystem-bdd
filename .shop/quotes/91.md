### the session, 2026-11-25T20:31:55.599000+00:00, d6db5e56-6421-4dfa-b760-5cab110cfe4d

```text
I'd keep it to three things, not two. Jobs and the domain map are the problem space, but without a third piece connecting them to the bounded contexts, nothing at the product level can be checked.

**The minimum product layer**

1. **Product identity.** The product's id (it's already the root of every context id), its purpose (the business problem in one or two sentences), and its actors (who it serves). Today's context ids already hang off a product id, `missingmass.io/shopsystem/...`, and decision 0129 makes the product the root of context ids. So this mostly makes explicit something that already exists.

2. **Jobs to be done.** Each job has an id, the actor whose job it is, and a statement: "When ‹situation›, ‹actor› wants ‹motivation›, so they can ‹outcome›." Jobs describe outcomes, not features, so they stay stable when the solution changes. That's what makes them the right anchor for a product.

3. **The domain map, with the context map folded in.**
   - **Subdomains:** each has its type (core, supporting or generic; decision 0128 already puts this here) and the jobs it serves.
   - **Bounded contexts:** each with the subdomains it addresses (many-to-many) and the shop that hosts it.
   - **Relations between contexts:** published contract, adapter spec, or customer/supplier. These are mostly not declared by hand: obtained contracts, driven ports and requests already say who is upstream of whom, so the map can be derived and checked against the contexts.

**Why the third piece earns its place:** it gives a trace from job to subdomain to context to capability. If each capability says which jobs it serves, the checks become possible:
- a job that no capability serves is a gap;
- a capability that serves no job is either supporting work or waste;
- a subdomain that no context addresses is unsolved.

Without that link, jobs and subdomains are just documents.
```
