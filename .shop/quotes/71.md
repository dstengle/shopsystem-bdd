```text
In part. As planned, the upstream contract sits beside the downstream spec rather than inside it, and it is only partly used. The plan also makes a choice it never states, and I think it's the wrong one.

**How the plan has the downstream use it**

The upstream contract would be kept under `spec/upstreams/<id>/` as a read-only layer, much like a pack. It brings in the upstream's words and operations, which serve four purposes:

1. **Downstream lines can parse the upstream's words.** In the test, "When the person writes an entry with a title, the journal service shall add a note" parsed only because "add" and "note" came from the copied upstream.
2. **`spec logic` reasons across both contexts.** The test found a real clash: journal#r3 counts on the upstream completing a request that the upstream's notes#r2 refuses.
3. **Readiness checks what a line relies on.** A downstream line that needs something the contract doesn't promise is flagged, and you fall back to `work request`.
4. **It is never the downstream's to build.** No features are generated from it, its ids are never renumbered, and v_a1 is not raised for it.

So it doesn't fit as capabilities. Its requirements are the upstream's behaviour, not obligations this context builds. Keeping it as a layer handles that part well enough.

**Where it doesn't fit**

The plan pulls the upstream's vocabulary straight into the downstream's grammar, so downstream lines speak the upstream's language. That makes the downstream a conformist without anyone having decided it. It also conflicts with our own rule that accepted lines are written "in this context's own terms." With a direct import:
- the journal context's requirements say "add a note" instead of something in its own terms, such as "keep an entry";
- any rename or reshape upstream breaks the downstream's lines, not just its wiring;
- the plan has no place for a port. Directive-12 asked how ports link to the core, and the plan doesn't answer that.
```
