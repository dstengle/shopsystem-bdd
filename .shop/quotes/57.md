### the session, 2026-11-19T17:00:26.747000+00:00, 8e207643-5291-47c4-a4a2-055ba7bcb0f9

```text
I'd split "why the grammar changed" into three kinds of why. Each lives in a place where it can't drift.

**1. What a construct is for.** This is part of the definition, so it goes in the construct's catalogue entry as a short "Purpose" field, next to its form and example. For example: "`holds-exactly`: says a container holds exactly the things that meet a condition, so a line can state a complete set." Change the construct and you edit the same entry, so the purpose and the definition can't diverge.

**2. Where a change came from.** This is history, but structured history. The catalogue entry carries references rather than prose:
- `Since: b274`
- `Changed: b297, b315`

Those items are already structured artifacts: each one holds the person's quote, its shaping note and its lines. So the "why" for a change is found by following a link through the graph, not stored as a copy of it. Two safeguards keep the links honest:
- the spec check verifies that each referenced item exists and was delivered;
- the batch that changes a construct must add its item to `Changed:`.

The commits already cite their item, so that check is mechanical. Records stay out of it, as the policy says.

**3. Choices that hold across the whole language.** Examples: "the grammar has no passive", "actors are proper nouns", "a line has one meaning or is refused". These are real decisions: constraints on every construct, made for reasons. They belong in design-decisions, at tier `check`, each enforced by refusal examples in the corpus. The drift you're worried about can't happen here, because if the grammar starts accepting a passive, the decision's own test fails.

So no decision is written per change. Decisions cover only constraints across the whole language, and those are enforced by tests. Everything specific to one construct stays in its catalogue entry.
```
